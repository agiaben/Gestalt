Gestalt Psychology

A school of psychology that emphasizes that we perceive objects as whole patterns rather than as the sum of their individual parts. The whole is different from — and greater than — the sum of its parts.

Founded ~1910 Germany Perception & Cognition
"The whole is other than the sum of the parts."
— Kurt Koffka, 1935
Core idea

Gestalt psychologists argued against the structuralist view that perception is built up from elementary sensations. Instead, they showed that the brain actively organizes sensory input into meaningful, coherent wholes using innate organizational laws.

Key contributions
Perceptual organization

Identified laws of perceptual grouping (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common fate) that explain how we naturally organize visual elements.

Insight learning

Köhler's experiments with chimpanzees demonstrated that problem-solving can occur through sudden "aha!" moments of insight rather than gradual trial-and-error.

Phi phenomenon

Wertheimer's discovery that rapid alternation of two stationary lights creates the illusion of motion — a foundational finding for film and animation.

The three founders

MW Max Wertheimer

1880 – 1943
Considered the father of Gestalt psychology. His 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon launched the movement.

Key work: "Experimental Studies on the Perception of Movement" (1912)

Wertheimer observed that when two lights flash in rapid succession, we perceive a single light moving between them — not two separate lights. This "apparent motion" proved that perception is an active, organizational process, not a passive accumulation of sensations. He later fled Nazi Germany and taught at the New School for Social Research in New York.

WK Wolfgang Köhler

1887 – 1967
Pioneered insight learning research with chimpanzees on Tenerife during World War I.

Key work: "The Mentality of Apes" (1917 / 1925)

Köhler conducted famous experiments with Sultan the chimpanzee, showing that apes could solve problems through insight rather than trial-and-error. He also introduced the concept of "isomorphism" — the idea that brain processes mirror the structure of perceived phenomena. He later became a vocal critic of Nazi ideology and emigrated to the United States.

KK Kurt Koffka

1886 – 1941
The systematizer who brought Gestalt ideas to American psychology through his influential textbook.

Key work: "The Growth of the Mind" (1924) and "Principles of Gestalt Psychology" (1935)

Koffka extended Gestalt principles beyond perception to child development, memory, and learning. He argued that behavior must be studied as a "behavioral field" organized according to Gestalt laws. His 1935 textbook remains the most comprehensive statement of Gestalt theory. He taught at Smith College in Massachusetts.
How they met

1910 — Frankfurt train

Wertheimer purchased a toy stroboscope on a train and realized it could be used to study apparent motion. He got off at Frankfurt and recruited Koffka and Köhler — then students at the Psychological Institute — as his research assistants.

Origin story

1912 — The phi phenomenon

Wertheimer published his landmark paper, establishing the empirical basis for Gestalt psychology. Koffka and Köhler became his collaborators and co-founders of the new school.

Founding paper

1913–1920 — Tenerife station

Köhler directed the Prussian Academy of Sciences anthropoid research station on Tenerife, where he conducted his famous chimpanzee experiments on insight learning.

Köhler's apes

1920s–30s — Migration to America

All three eventually emigrated to the United States due to rising anti-Semitism and the Nazi regime, bringing Gestalt psychology to American academia.

Laws of Perceptual Organization

The brain automatically organizes visual information according to these innate principles. Click each card to see a visual explanation.

1. Proximity

Elements that are close together are perceived as belonging together.

2. Similarity

Elements that look alike (shape, color, size) are grouped together.

3. Closure

The mind fills in missing information to perceive complete, familiar shapes.

4. Continuity

The eye follows smooth, continuous paths rather than abrupt changes in direction.

5. Figure-Ground

We automatically separate a visual scene into a foreground figure and a background.

6. Common Fate

Elements that move or change in the same direction are perceived as a group.

The phi phenomenon (Wertheimer, 1912)

Wertheimer set up two slits in a tachistoscope. When light flashed through the first slit, then the second slit in rapid succession, observers did not see two separate lights — they saw one light moving from one position to the other. This "apparent motion" proved that perception is an active, organizational process.

Why it matters

The phi phenomenon is the basis for all motion pictures and animation. Film is simply a series of still images presented rapidly enough that the brain perceives continuous motion — a Gestalt organizational process, not a physical reality.
